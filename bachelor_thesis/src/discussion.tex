\chapter{Discussion\label{chap:discussion}}
% This thesis set out to build an automated system for classifying the quality of news articles. The final chosen model performed well overall, indicating that the approach is sound. The system correctly distinguished between the predefined quality levels (Insufficient, Short but Sufficient, Standard, High-Quality). The model’s predictions closely matched the categories that human editors assigned, showing that it learned to apply the editorial criteria consistently. In particular, the combination of a transformer-based language model (DeBERTaV3) and carefully engineered features proved effective in capturing important aspects of article quality, such as readability, structure, and presence of references. 

% The model showed several strengths. It handled the class imbalance in the data through techniques (as described in earlier chapters) so that even the rare \ac{Class 3} was recognized reasonably well. The engineered features based on journalistic standards (such as measures of textual complexity, citation use, and article structure) appeared to contribute positively, as the model could detect the intended quality signals. Some confusion still remained between adjacent classes (for instance, telling apart a \ac{Class 2} from \ac{Class 3} one), but this is also a difficulty for human editors. Overall, the results indicate that automated classification can largely mimic human judgments.

The hybrid model that combined transformer-based text representations with a \ac{CatBoost} classifier achieved the highest performance among all tested approaches. On the held-out test set, this ensemble obtained a weighted F1 score of approximately 0.78 and an accuracy around 78\%. This substantially outperformed both the standalone transformer models and the standalone feature-based classifier, confirming that the fusion of deep contextual language features with manually engineered linguistic/structural features improves classification accuracy. 

Notably, the hybrid system’s predictions are \textit{nearly on par with the judgments of human editors}, approaching the estimated human inter-annotator agreement upper bound of roughly 80\%. In practical terms, the model has achieved a level of performance close to human-level consistency in evaluating article quality. Beyond the numeric metrics, the results provide insight into why the hybrid approach was beneficial. The transformer component (\ac{DeBERTaV3}) contributed a rich understanding of article content and context, while the \ac{CatBoost} component leveraged this alongside additional features (e.g. structural and linguistic cues) to make final decisions. The \ac{CatBoost} classifier effectively used the transformer’s output (the four-class logit scores) as extra inputs to correct certain mistakes the transformer alone would make.

It is important to note that while the hybrid model delivered superior results, the margin over the best transformer-only model was not vast (DeBERTaV3 large with features have F1 weighted score around 0.766). This indicates that the transformer already captured much of the signal, and the engineered features provided a complementary but smaller boost. The remaining confusion between mid-tier quality classes suggests that some nuances of quality assessment are inherently challenging, even with the combined approach. Nevertheless, achieving nearly 0.78 F1 weighted with four-class classification is a significant outcome – it demonstrates the feasibility of automated quality classification. 

The hybrid model not only attained the best quantitative performance but also did so in a way that aligns closely with human-like judgments, underlining its value for the intended application.

\section{Practical Impact}
The success of the model has clear practical implications for media platforms. In the case of Seznam.cz, article submissions have been increasing by roughly 50\% per year, a pace that makes manual review of all content infeasible. Additionally, human evaluations can be inconsistent between editors, leading to variability in what is deemed “quality” content. The developed classifier offers a solution by handling every incoming article in real-time with uniform criteria, thereby increasing scalability, reducing the manual review burden, and ensuring consistent quality standards across the platform. Key benefits of integrating this model include:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Automated Quality Control at Scale:} Every submitted article can be evaluated automatically, providing real-time assessment for 100\% of content. This allows the platform to keep up with high submission rates and growth in content volume while maintaining oversight of quality.
  \item \textbf{Increased Consistency:} The model applies a single learned set of quality criteria derived from expert annotations, ensuring that the rating of articles is consistent and not subject to individual subjective differences. This uniform application of editorial guidelines means that all content is judged by the same standards, which is essential for fairness and brand integrity.
  \item \textbf{Content Quality and User Trust:} By catching substandard content automatically, the platform can prevent low-quality articles from reaching the front page or recommendation feeds. This ensures content consistency and protects the platform’s credibility, as unchecked poor-quality content (e.g. lacking depth or containing errors) could harm user trust and the outlet’s reputation.
\end{itemize}

These benefits illustrate that the classifier is not just a theoretical exercise, but a practically valuable tool. The potential of this automated system to support editorial decision-making and ensure content quality at scale has been clearly demonstrated by the results. 

In summary, the hybrid model adds tangible value by making the editorial process more scalable, efficient, and consistent, directly aligning with the needs of a large-scale news platform like Seznam.cz.

\chapter{Future Work\label{chap:future_work}}

While the developed quality classification system has proven effective, there are several avenues for future work to further enhance its performance and usefulness. 

A top priority is to validate the model’s impact in a real-world setting through controlled deployment experiments. Applying an A/B testing framework would be an ideal next step to measure how the model influences user engagement and editorial outcomes once integrated into the live platform. In such a test, a portion of users or articles would be subject to the model’s intervention, while a control group would experience the status quo without the model’s filtering. This experimentation will allow precise measurement of the model’s benefits: for example, comparing user engagement metrics between the two groups. 

Additionally, editorial accuracy and efficiency should be evaluated post-deployment – for instance, tracking how often editors override the model’s decisions or how the agreement rate between the model’s predictions and editors’ judgments improves over time. If the model is truly capturing the editors’ criteria well, one would expect high agreement and a reduction in manual corrections needed.

Another important direction for future work is to refine the model further and address its current limitations. Although the hybrid model approaches human-level performance, the confusion between adjacent quality categories (particularly between Class 1 and Class 2 articles) remains an area for improvement. Future research could explore techniques to better distinguish borderline cases – for example, by introducing additional features focused on depth of content and novelty to separate \ac{Class 1} pieces from \ac{Class 2}. Gathering more labeled data in the under-represented high-quality category or applying data augmentation could also help the model learn finer distinctions. 

In conclusion, the developed hybrid classification model has proven its merit both in terms of performance and practical applicability. The discussion above underscores that combining transformer-based language understanding with feature-based insights was a fruitful strategy, yielding near-human accuracy in classifying article quality. Equally important, it has the potential to substantially improve editorial workflows at scale – automating quality control, enhancing consistency, and allowing platforms like Seznam.cz to uphold high journalistic standards amid growing content demands. The next phase is to build on this foundation: rigorously test the system’s impact via A/B tests and real-world deployment, refine it based on those findings, and continue innovating so that automated tools can reliably support human editors in delivering quality content. This future-facing approach will ensure that the classifier not only meets academic benchmarks but also delivers sustained value in the dynamic environment of digital journalism.
